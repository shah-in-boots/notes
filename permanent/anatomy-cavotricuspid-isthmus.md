# Anatomical considerations

@Gami2010 is a paper by Asirvatham that help us troubleshoot issues with catheter manipulation along the CTI.

From the paper itself, in [Gami2010](literature/Gami2010.md), in regards to __sub-Eustachian pouches__...

> We found a distinct sub-Eustachian pouch in 16% of adult hearts. The pouches were always bound by 3 valvular structures: the Eustachian valve, the TV, and the tricuspid valve  

> They were never found in the lateral third of the CVTI. This finding may have relevance for ablationists, since when a sub-Eustachian pouch is suspected (due to catheter instability or findings from right atrial angiography or intracardiac ultrasound), the CVTI ablation line may be performed more laterally to avoid this pouch.  

![LAO view showing the sub-Eustachian pouch that exists more on the CS/septal side instead of hte more lateral side (this one is quite large)](figures/Gami2010/fig-3-x285-y547.png)

They also described the role of pectinate muscle, which cross the CTI from the lateral side, but diminish as they move septally and when closer to the CS. 
  
> These findings provide electrophysiologists an anatomic basis to troubleshoot difficulty with ablation of CVTI-AFL. If challenged by sites where high amplitude signals (from pectinate muscle ridges) alternate with abrupt impedance rises and other suggestions of low flow states (from the crevices between pectinate muscles),20-22 the ablationist may choose to perform CVTI ablation more septally, where the pectinate muscles are less prominent.
