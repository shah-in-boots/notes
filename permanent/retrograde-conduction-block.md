Retrograde block is primarily in the AV node.
Normally conduction goes up either through fast (AVJ) or slow (proximal CS), but there can be block below in the HSP (rare).

Retrograde conduction block:

- burst pacing = $AVN >> HSP$
- programmed extrastimuli = $HSP > AVN$ as it takes the $HSP$ several beats to adapt

Below we have an example of bidirectional RBBB. 
Anterograde sinus beat shows native RBBB, but when V-pacing, the stim-H time is very prolonged, suggesting myocardial capture to get to retrograde His.
This indicates retrograde RBBB. 
However, with the S2, can see that hte His, although prolongs further, does not go up to the A, so alos retrograde AVN block.

![Complex example of multi-level block. Seen with the anterograde RBB, retrograde RBB, and retrograde AVN all in one VEST protocol](resources/retrograde-conduction-block.png)

Another example of retrograde conduction block. 
The block happens in the level of the AV node, which we can tell because the first conducted AH after a V-paced beat is longer than the next AH, meaning that there was concealed conduction into the AV node.

![Retrograde AVN block (versus block in RBB) due to concealed conduction into the AV node](resources/retrograde-conduction-block-1.png)

How is conduction happening retrograde in the following example?
We can see His-dependence on the stim-CS timing (e.g. the HA is roughly the same even with extrastimuli), confiming that there is conduction over the AV node.
However, the HRA position is much earlier on the first 2 beats, and changes or delays to have concentric activation on the last beat.
In other words, the first two beats have eccentric atrial activation, which blocks and becomes concentric on the S2.
That suggests there was a right lateral accessory pathway conducting the first two beats.
