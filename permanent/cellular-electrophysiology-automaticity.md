Pacemaker activity from a cellular perspective is limited to primarily the sinus node, the AV node (in part), infra-nodal conduction system.
Most normal myocytes don't have this property.

What is unique is that they have a consistent pattern of depolarization during phase IV that allows them to work as pacemaker cells (giving automaticity). 

Phase IV has gradual increase in membrane potentials. Mechanism is through increasing net inward current
- $I_{Kf}$ from the HCN4 gene
- $I_{CaT}$ ($Ca^{++}$ current, T-type)
- NCX exchanger channels
- pathological conditions essentially end up with abnormal intracellular $Ca^{++}$ cycling

In pacemaker tissue, the threshold for excitation (when calcium channels open), deterines the firing rate.  
Firing rate can be effected by multiple different situations:
- If $\uparrow$ threshold for excitation, e.g. hypercalcemia, calcium channel blockers, then firing rate will go down
- If $\downarrow$ phase IV slope, then will take longer until next firing, occurs in setting of hyperkalemia, beta-blockers, ivabradine
- If phase III becomes more negative, then rate will slow down because will take longer to reach excitation threshold, happens with acetylcholine, adenosine
- If $\uparrow$ APD, then can delay phase IV leading to slower firing rate, through $K^+$ blocking drugs
